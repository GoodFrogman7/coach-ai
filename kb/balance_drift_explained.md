# Balance Drift: Stability During Strokes

## Common Question Phrasing

This section covers: What causes balance drift? Why am I losing balance during strokes? What is lateral movement or COM drift? How do I stop swaying sideways? Why am I leaning sideways when hitting? What causes center of mass drift?

## What Is Balance Drift?

Balance drift measures lateral (side-to-side) movement of your center of mass (COM) during the stroke. Excessive drift, also called sway or leaning sideways, indicates instability and reduces power transfer. When you lose balance during the stroke, your body drifts laterally instead of staying grounded.

## Why Stability Matters

### Power Transfer
- Stable base → efficient energy transfer to ball
- Drifting base → energy lost in lateral movement
- Result: Drift = weaker, less consistent shots

### Consistency
- Stable contact point → predictable ball flight
- Drifting contact → variable outcomes
- Result: Drift = inconsistency

### Recovery Speed
- Stable finish → quick recovery
- Drifting finish → extra time to regain balance
- Result: Drift = slower court coverage

## Measuring Balance Drift

Coach AI tracks your center of mass movement:
- **Minimal drift**: <5cm lateral movement
- **Moderate drift**: 5-10cm
- **Excessive drift**: >10cm

Stability score (0-100): Higher = more stable

## Common Causes of Balance Drift

Quick summary - Balance drift happens when:
- **Stance too narrow** (feet too close = unstable base)
- **Poor weight transfer** (losing balance, momentum carries you sideways)
- **Weak core** (torso sways, can't stabilize)
- **Overreaching** (stretching too far, leaning sideways)
- **Fatigue** (tired muscles can't maintain stability)

### Detailed Causes

### 1. Stance Too Narrow
**Problem**: Feet too close together
**Result**: Unstable base, easy to knock off balance, causes lateral drift
**Fix**: Widen stance to 2-2.5 feet

### 2. Poor Weight Transfer
**Problem**: Excessive weight shift without control
**Result**: Momentum carries you off balance, swaying sideways
**Fix**: Controlled weight transfer, front foot plants firmly

### 3. Weak Core
**Problem**: Core can't stabilize trunk
**Result**: Torso sways during stroke, center of mass drifts
**Fix**: Core strengthening exercises

### 4. Overreaching
**Problem**: Stretching too far for ball
**Result**: Off-balance contact, leaning sideways
**Fix**: Better court positioning, earlier preparation

### 5. Fatigue
**Problem**: Tired stabilizer muscles
**Result**: Progressive drift increases through session
**Fix**: Conditioning + manage training load

## How to Fix Balance Drift

**Immediate fixes:**
- Widen your stance (2-2.5 feet)
- Engage core before every stroke
- Plant front foot firmly during contact
- Stop overreaching for balls

**Training fixes:**
- Core strengthening (planks, side planks, rotational exercises)
- Balance drills (single-leg exercises, wobble board)
- Proper footwork patterns
- Video analysis to see your drift patterns

## Technical Corrections

### Stance Width
- **Check**: Feet 2-2.5 feet apart at contact
- **Feel**: Stable, grounded, athletic position
- **Drill**: Shadow swings with exaggerated wide stance

### Weight Distribution
- **Check**: 60% front foot, 40% back foot at contact
- **Feel**: Balanced, not falling forward/backward
- **Drill**: Contact position holds (freeze at contact for 3 seconds)

### Core Engagement
- **Check**: Abs tight, trunk stable
- **Feel**: Solid, connected feeling
- **Drill**: Planks, side planks, rotational exercises

### Head Position
- **Check**: Head level, not bobbing
- **Feel**: Eyes level with horizon
- **Drill**: Balance beam practice, eyes closed shadow swings

## Physical Training

### Strengthening
- **Core**: Planks, bird dogs, dead bugs
- **Legs**: Squats, lunges (especially lateral lunges)
- **Ankles**: Single-leg balance, wobble board

### Balance Training
- **Static**: Single-leg stands, eyes closed balance
- **Dynamic**: Balance board strokes, unstable surface practice
- **Reactive**: Reaction ball catches while on one leg

### Proprioception
- **Eyes closed drills**: Shadow swings without visual feedback
- **Unstable surfaces**: BOSU ball, balance board
- **Perturbation training**: Partner gently pushes during shadow swings

## Session Patterns

### Early Session (Fresh)
- Minimal drift expected
- Good stability and control
- Baseline measurement

### Mid Session
- Slight drift increase acceptable
- Maintain stability focus
- Monitor for excessive change

### Late Session (Fatigued)
- Drift typically increases 20-40%
- If >40% increase → fatigue is high
- Consider ending session or reducing intensity

## Drift vs Technique

### Technique-Driven Drift
- Present from first stroke
- Consistent pattern (always same direction/magnitude)
- Improves with focus and awareness
- Correctable through technical adjustment

### Fatigue-Driven Drift
- Increases progressively during session
- Variable pattern (random directions)
- Doesn't improve with focus when tired
- Requires rest/recovery

## Integration with Coach AI

When Coach AI flags balance drift:
1. **Check technique first**: Stance width, weight transfer, core engagement
2. **Assess fatigue**: Is it worse late in session?
3. **Review trends**: Getting better or worse over sessions?
4. **Adjust training**: More balance work or more recovery

## Realistic Expectations

Perfect stability is rare. Acceptable targets:
- **Elite**: <3cm drift, stability score >85
- **Advanced**: <5cm drift, stability score >75
- **Intermediate**: <8cm drift, stability score >65
- **Development**: <10cm drift, stability score >55

Progress gradually. Even 10-20% improvement is significant.

## Drills Specifically for Balance

1. **Single-leg shadow swings**: Execute backhand on one leg
2. **Balance board strokes**: Shadow swings on unstable surface
3. **Freeze drills**: Hold contact position for 5 seconds, check stability
4. **Eyes closed practice**: Remove visual feedback to enhance proprioception
5. **Perturbation drills**: Partner pushes gently during stroke, resist drift

Consistent practice builds the neuromuscular patterns for automatic stability.
